California researchers have documented the deaths of 73 pelicans attributed to river otters in the Point Reyes National Seashore lagoon. The findings stem from 19 survey expeditions conducted by the research teams, which observed an unusual predator-prey interaction in the sensitive coastal ecosystem. The observations indicate a notable instance of river otters preying on large avian species, an interaction that has also led to observable behavioral adaptations among the pelican population.

The study, originating from research efforts in the Point Reyes area, highlights an unexpected predatory dynamic. While river otters (Lontra canadensis) are known carnivores, their diet primarily consists of fish, crustaceans, and small mammals. Large birds like pelicans are not typical prey, making this concentration of pelican mortality noteworthy for wildlife ecologists. The consistent surveys provided a robust dataset, allowing researchers to quantify the extent of this predation over a defined period.

Point Reyes National Seashore, a protected area north of San Francisco, California, is a critical habitat for numerous wildlife species, including migratory and resident birds. The lagoon environment offers a rich feeding ground for pelicans, primarily brown pelicans (Pelecanus occidentalis), which are common in the region. The close proximity and interaction between the two species within the confined lagoon setting appear to have facilitated these predatory events.

Key observations from the research include:

*   **Total Deaths:** 73 pelicans were documented as killed by river otters.
*   **Survey Frequency:** Researchers conducted 19 separate surveys of the lagoon to compile the data.
*   **Location:** All documented incidents occurred within the Point Reyes National Seashore lagoon.
*   **Behavioral Adaptation:** A significant finding is that some pelicans have begun exhibiting avoidance behavior, flying away when river otters approach. This suggests a rapid learning and adaptation process within the pelican population in response to the predation threat.

The documentation of these events underscores the complex and ever-changing nature of predator-prey relationships within ecosystems. The observed shift in pelican behavior, from being potentially vulnerable to actively avoiding otters, demonstrates the speed at which species can adapt to new threats. Researchers will likely continue monitoring the Point Reyes lagoon to better understand the long-term ecological implications of this interaction, including potential impacts on local pelican populations and the river otter's dietary habits. The findings contribute valuable data to the understanding of wildlife ecology and interspecies dynamics in coastal environments.
