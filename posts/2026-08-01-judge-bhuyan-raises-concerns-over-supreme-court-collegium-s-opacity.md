Judge Bhuyan recently brought the transparency of the Supreme Court (SC) collegium system into question, reigniting public discourse surrounding the process of judicial appointments in India. The remarks from Judge Bhuyan highlight long-standing concerns regarding the collegium's operational methods and the perceived lack of public insight into its decisions. This development underscores the persistent debate within legal and political circles over the accountability and criteria applied in selecting judges for the nation's higher judiciary.

The Supreme Court collegium, comprising the Chief Justice of India and four senior-most judges of the apex court, is responsible for recommending the appointment and transfer of judges to the High Courts and the Supreme Court. While designed to uphold judicial independence by keeping executive interference at bay, the system has frequently faced criticism for its perceived "opacity" and the absence of clear, publicly articulated guidelines for its selections. Critics argue that the non-disclosure of minutes, criteria, and reasons behind specific recommendations hinders transparency and public accountability.

The questioning by Judge Bhuyan carries significant weight, as it originates from within the judiciary itself. Such internal scrutiny brings renewed attention to the structural mechanisms governing judicial appointments. The criticisms typically revolve around several key areas:

*   **Lack of Public Criteria:** There are no officially published, specific criteria that detail how candidates are evaluated for judicial appointments or transfers.
*   **Absence of Written Records:** Decisions of the collegium are often not accompanied by detailed written justifications or minutes, making it difficult to understand the rationale behind specific choices.
*   **Limited Accountability:** Without clear guidelines and transparent processes, evaluating the collegium's decisions and holding it accountable becomes challenging.
*   **Perception of Favouritism:** The lack of transparency can lead to perceptions of subjectivity or favouritism, potentially eroding public trust in the independence and fairness of the judiciary.

The recurring debate over the collegium's transparency has periodically led to calls for judicial reforms, with various proposals ranging from the establishment of a National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) – which was struck down by the Supreme Court in 2015 – to implementing more detailed public disclosure norms for collegium decisions. The court itself has, in the past, acknowledged the need for greater transparency, leading to partial steps such as publishing collegium resolutions on the Supreme Court's website. However, the extent of detail provided has remained a point of contention.

Judge Bhuyan's recent comments are likely to intensify discussions among legal practitioners, policymakers, and the public regarding the future of judicial appointments. While the immediate implications are uncertain, the statement reinforces the ongoing pressure for greater openness in the functioning of one of the nation's most critical institutions. Further developments are expected as stakeholders continue to engage with the complex issues surrounding judicial independence, transparency, and the integrity of the appointment process.
