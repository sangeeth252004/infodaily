Fifty years ago, in 1974, Spain imported approximately two tons of American red swamp crayfish (*Procambarus clarkii*) with the intent of bolstering its aquaculture industry. The species, also known as the Louisiana crayfish, was introduced primarily for human consumption and economic development. Half a century later, this initial commercial venture has resulted in a widespread ecological challenge, with the species now deeply entrenched across Spanish wetlands and rivers, deemed impossible to eradicate by conservationists and authorities.

The original consignment of *P. clarkii* was brought from Louisiana, United States, and initially introduced to the Guadalquivir marshes in Andalusia and subsequently to other regions such as Valencia. The decision was driven by the species' rapid growth rate, adaptability, and perceived high market value, offering a new protein source and export potential for Spain. Early aquaculture efforts focused on controlled environments, but the crayfish soon demonstrated an alarming ability to escape and colonize adjacent natural habitats.

*Procambarus clarkii* possesses several biological traits that facilitated its rapid expansion and establishment as an invasive species. It exhibits a high reproductive capacity, reaching sexual maturity quickly and producing multiple clutches of eggs annually. Its omnivorous diet allows it to thrive in diverse environments, consuming aquatic plants, insects, fish eggs, and even small vertebrates. Furthermore, the species is highly adaptable to varying water conditions, including droughts and poor water quality, and its burrowing habits allow it to survive adverse conditions by digging into riverbeds and banks, which also damages infrastructure like irrigation canals and rice field dikes.

The ecological consequences for Spain's ecosystems have been significant. The American red swamp crayfish directly competes with native European crayfish species, such as the white-clawed crayfish (*Austropotamobius pallipes*), which are already endangered. Beyond competition, *P. clarkii* is a carrier of the crayfish plague (*Aphanomyces astaci*), a disease to which it is largely immune but which is lethal to native European crayfish populations. Its prolific feeding habits also disrupt aquatic food webs, reducing biodiversity by consuming amphibian eggs, tadpoles, and juvenile fish, and altering vegetation composition. Economically, its burrowing activities have caused damage to agricultural infrastructure, particularly in rice-growing regions.

Over the decades, numerous attempts have been made to control the populations of *P. clarkii*. However, its biological resilience and widespread distribution have rendered eradication efforts largely ineffective. The species is now classified as an Invasive Alien Species (IAS) across the European Union, with strict regulations prohibiting its introduction, breeding, transport, and release. Current management strategies in Spain focus primarily on containment, mitigation of its impacts, and localized control efforts rather than complete removal, which is no longer considered a viable objective.

The enduring presence of the American red swamp crayfish in Spain serves as a prominent case study in the long-term ecological and economic challenges posed by introducing non-native species for commercial purposes. Fifty years after its initial import, the species has irrevocably altered Spanish aquatic ecosystems, underscoring the complexities and irreversible nature of biological invasions and the critical need for rigorous risk assessment in aquaculture and biodiversity management. Ongoing research continues to explore sustainable management practices to coexist with this established invader.
