**Washington D.C.** – A recent study analyzing decades of inspection data has revealed that US inspectors recorded 2,562 interceptions of woodwasps at ports of entry between 1952 and 2020. The research, conducted by Houping Liu of the USDA Forest Service, identified wood packaging material (WPM) as the primary pathway for these insects, which pose a significant threat to forest ecosystems and agricultural industries. The findings were published in the journal *Forest Science*.

The comprehensive analysis spanning nearly seven decades underscores the persistent challenge of preventing the introduction of non-native insect species through international trade. Woodwasps, including species such as the European woodwasp (*Sirex noctilio*), are known for their ability to bore into trees, causing damage that can range from reduced timber quality to tree mortality. The introduction of such pests can lead to substantial economic losses for forestry sectors and may necessitate costly eradication or management programs.

The study detailed several key findings regarding these interceptions:

*   **Pathway Dominance:** Wooden packaging materials, including pallets, crates, dunnage, and other shipping components, accounted for the vast majority of woodwasp interceptions. These materials often originate from regions where these pests are endemic.
*   **Species Diversity:** More than two dozen species of exotic woodwasps were intercepted during the study period, with most originating from Eurasia. Commonly intercepted species included *Tremex longicollis* and *Sirex noctilio*.
*   **Interception Peaks:** The research indicated that interceptions of woodwasps peaked during the 1990s, prior to the widespread implementation of stricter international phytosanitary standards.
*   **Impact of ISPM 15:** A notable decline in woodwasp interceptions was observed after 2005, following the global implementation of the International Standards for Phytosanitary Measures No. 15 (ISPM 15). This international regulation mandates that all solid wood packaging material used in international trade must be heat-treated or fumigated to kill pests.

Despite the positive impact of ISPM 15, which significantly reduced the influx of woodwasps, the study noted that interceptions continue to occur. This indicates ongoing challenges in full compliance, effective enforcement, or the adaptation of pests to existing treatment methods. The continued detection of these insects highlights the necessity for sustained vigilance and robust inspection protocols at US borders.

The research emphasizes the critical role of international cooperation and continued adherence to phytosanitary regulations in mitigating the risks associated with global trade. Moving forward, the findings are expected to inform policy decisions and operational strategies aimed at strengthening biosecurity measures and protecting domestic forest resources from invasive woodwasp species.
