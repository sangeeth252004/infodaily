NEW DELHI – The Uddhav Thackeray faction of Shiv Sena recently presented its arguments before the Supreme Court of India, asserting that the act of political defection by a group of legislators should not be legitimized or rewarded with control over the original party. The submissions were made as part of the ongoing legal battle concerning the split within the Shiv Sena and the legitimacy of the Eknath Shinde-led government in Maharashtra.

During the proceedings, senior counsel representing the Uddhav Thackeray faction contended that allowing defecting members to claim the party's name and symbol would undermine the core principles of the Tenth Schedule of the Constitution, commonly known as the Anti-Defection Law. The counsel emphasized that the law was enacted to prevent political horse-trading and maintain parliamentary stability, arguing that any interpretation allowing defections to be rewarded would create a dangerous precedent for Indian democracy.

The arguments centered on the premise that the original political party, not the legislative party, holds the ultimate authority and that members who defect lose their moral and legal right to represent the party. The Uddhav camp highlighted that the Speaker of the legislative assembly has a crucial role in disqualifying defecting members, and a floor test cannot validate actions taken in contravention of the anti-defection provisions. The counsel stressed that the "sin of defection," as they termed it, should not be permissible under constitutional frameworks, especially when it results in a change of government and party control.

Key points raised by the Uddhav Thackeray faction included:
*   **Upholding the Anti-Defection Law:** The Tenth Schedule aims to deter floor-crossing and should be strictly enforced to preserve the integrity of political parties.
*   **Party Authority vs. Legislative Wing:** The primary political party's authority to command loyalty and maintain its identity must be protected over a breakaway legislative group.
*   **Invalidation of Defection by Floor Test:** A legislative floor test, while demonstrating majority support in the assembly, cannot retrospectively legitimize the act of defection from the parent party.
*   **Speaker's Role in Disqualification:** The Speaker's constitutional duty to rule on disqualification petitions against defecting MLAs is paramount and should not be circumvented.

The Supreme Court bench, comprising of senior judges, heard detailed submissions from both sides in the complex matter that has significant implications for party democracy and the interpretation of anti-defection laws in India. The dispute originated in June 2022 when a large group of Shiv Sena MLAs, led by Eknath Shinde, rebelled against Uddhav Thackeray's leadership, leading to the collapse of the Maha Vikas Aghadi government and the formation of a new administration.

The court's decision in this case is highly anticipated, as it will clarify the constitutional boundaries concerning party splits, the powers of the Speaker, and the scope of the anti-defection law. The verdict is expected to have far-reaching consequences for political stability and the functional dynamics of multi-party democracy in India. The Supreme Court has reserved its judgment after concluding the hearings, indicating that a decision will be pronounced at a later date.
