Kenneth Deardorff, a Vietnam veteran, officially became the last person in United States history to receive a federal homestead title in 1988, concluding a 126-year federal land distribution program. Deardorff's 80-acre claim, located near the Stony River, approximately 100 miles west of Anchorage, Alaska, represented the final patent issued under the historic Homestead Act. His journey to secure the title began when he moved to Alaska in 1974.

The original Homestead Act of 1862 incentivized westward expansion by offering 160 acres of federal land to any adult citizen or intended citizen who would live on it, cultivate it, and make improvements for five years. This landmark legislation distributed over 270 million acres of public land, fundamentally shaping the demographic and agricultural landscape of the nation. While the Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976 effectively ended homesteading in the contiguous 48 states, an exception was made for Alaska. The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971 allowed for an extension of homesteading claims in the state until 1986, creating a final window of opportunity for individuals like Deardorff.

Deardorff moved to Alaska in 1974, initially seeking work and new opportunities. He subsequently learned about the remaining homesteading provisions and identified an 80-acre parcel of land suitable for a claim. To fulfill the stringent requirements for a federal land patent, Deardorff dedicated years to developing his remote property. His efforts included:

*   **Residence:** Establishing permanent residency on the land.
*   **Construction:** Building a cabin, which served as his primary dwelling.
*   **Agriculture:** Clearing land for cultivation and successfully growing crops such as potatoes and various vegetables.
*   **Livestock:** Raising small livestock, including pigs and chickens, to support his self-sufficient lifestyle.

These actions demonstrated his commitment to the land and met the federal government's criteria for improving and maintaining the property. After years of living on and developing the parcel, his application was processed, culminating in the issuance of his deed in 1988, nearly 14 years after his initial move to the state.

Deardorff's patent marked the official closure of an era that allowed millions of Americans to acquire land directly from the federal government. His successful claim underscores the enduring spirit of self-reliance and pioneering that characterized the homesteading movement. With the issuance of his title, the final chapter of the federal homesteading program, which spanned well over a century, concluded. There are currently no provisions for new federal homestead claims in the United States.
