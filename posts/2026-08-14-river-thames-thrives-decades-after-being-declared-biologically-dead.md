London’s River Thames, once officially declared "biologically dead" in 1957 due to severe pollution, has undergone a remarkable ecological recovery and now supports a diverse array of marine and estuarine life, including various species of sharks and seals. This transformation is the result of over six decades of dedicated environmental legislation, significant infrastructure investment, and sustained conservation efforts. The river, which flows through the heart of the UK capital, serves as a testament to successful urban waterway rehabilitation.

The mid-20th century saw the Thames reach its environmental nadir. Rapid industrialization and a burgeoning population in London led to the continuous discharge of raw sewage and industrial waste directly into the river. This overwhelming pollution depleted oxygen levels to critical lows, creating an anaerobic environment that was incapable of sustaining most aquatic life. The official declaration by the Natural History Museum underscored the ecological catastrophe, with common fish species disappearing and stretches of the river becoming devoid of oxygen.

The turning point for the Thames began in the late 1960s and 1970s with the implementation of stricter environmental regulations and the upgrading of sewage treatment facilities. Subsequent decades saw ongoing commitment, culminating in major infrastructure projects designed to intercept and treat wastewater before it reaches the river. A significant undertaking has been the construction of the Thames Tideway Tunnel, also known as London's "Super Sewer," a 25-kilometer (15.5-mile) super sewer designed to prevent millions of tonnes of raw sewage from overflowing into the Thames annually, particularly during heavy rainfall. The project, nearing completion, is set to further reduce pollution events substantially.

As a result of these persistent efforts, the river's health has demonstrably improved, leading to the return and proliferation of numerous species. Regular wildlife surveys conducted by organizations such as the Zoological Society of London (ZSL) and the Port of London Authority (PLA) document this ecological resurgence.

Key indicators of the Thames’ recovery include:
*   **Mammals:** Grey seals and common seals are now frequently observed along the estuary, with populations increasing year on year. Porpoises and even occasional dolphins have also been sighted further downstream.
*   **Fish:** Over 125 species of fish have been recorded, a substantial increase from the 1950s. These include European eels, bass, flounder, and migratory species like salmon and sea trout, which are gradually returning to the river system.
*   **Sharks:** Multiple shark species inhabit the Thames estuary, including tope sharks, starry smooth-hound sharks, and bull huss. These species are typically found in the brackish waters where the river meets the sea, indicating a healthy food web capable of supporting apex predators.
*   **Seahorses:** Both short-snouted and long-snouted seahorses have been documented in the estuary, highlighting the improved water quality and the presence of suitable habitats.
*   **Birds:** The return of diverse fish populations has, in turn, supported a broader range of waterfowl and wading birds, reflecting a more balanced ecosystem.

While the Thames' recovery is a celebrated environmental success story, conservation efforts remain ongoing. Challenges such as plastic pollution, microplastics, and the impact of climate change continue to pose threats to the river's long-term health. Monitoring programs, further infrastructure maintenance, and public engagement initiatives are crucial to ensure the continued vitality of the River Thames as a vibrant urban ecosystem.
