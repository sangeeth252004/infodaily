Lundy Island, located off the coast of Devon in the UK, has reported a dramatic increase in its seabird populations, including Atlantic puffins, following the successful eradication of non-native rats. The conservation effort, which began in 2002, has led to more than 40,000 seabirds now nesting on the island, marking a significant ecological recovery.

The project targeted black and brown rats, which were introduced to the island and posed a substantial threat to ground-nesting seabirds by preying on eggs and chicks. Prior to the eradication efforts, puffin numbers on Lundy had dwindled to just 13 individuals in 2000. Following the sustained conservation work, the puffin population has seen a substantial rebound, now numbering 1,335 breeding birds. This recovery is a key indicator of the broader environmental improvements on the island.

The absence of rats has created a safer breeding environment, allowing various seabird species to thrive. The island, managed by the National Trust and leased to the Landmark Trust, is a designated Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI) and a Marine Protected Area, making the success of this project particularly important for regional biodiversity. The rebound in bird numbers demonstrates the critical impact of removing invasive predators from vulnerable ecosystems.

Key details regarding Lundy Island's seabird recovery:
*   **Project Initiation:** The rat eradication program commenced in 2002.
*   **Puffin Population Pre-Eradication:** In the year 2000, only 13 Atlantic puffins were recorded on the island.
*   **Current Puffin Population:** The latest figures show 1,335 puffins now breeding on Lundy Island.
*   **Total Seabird Numbers:** Over 40,000 seabirds from various species currently nest on the island.
*   **Threat Addressed:** Non-native rats were identified as significant predators of seabird eggs and chicks.

The success of the Lundy Island project serves as a case study for island conservation efforts globally, demonstrating the effectiveness of targeted predator control in restoring threatened seabird colonies. Continued monitoring of the island’s bird populations and ecosystem health is expected to ensure the long-term sustainability of these gains, potentially influencing similar conservation strategies in other vulnerable island environments.
