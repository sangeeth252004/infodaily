The United States established the Federal Duck Stamp program in 1934, initially priced at just $1, as a direct funding mechanism for migratory bird and wetland conservation. This initiative, officially known as the Migratory Bird Hunting and Conservation Stamp, has since generated hundreds of millions of dollars, leading to the protection of more than six million acres of vital wildlife habitat across the nation.

Originating from the Migratory Bird Hunting Stamp Act, signed into law on March 16, 1934, the stamp was championed by prominent conservationists like J.N. "Ding" Darling, then chief of the U.S. Biological Survey (a predecessor to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, or USFWS). The legislation mandated that all waterfowl hunters aged 16 and older purchase an annual stamp, ensuring that those who directly benefit from healthy migratory bird populations also contribute to their sustenance.

The program's structure directs approximately 98 cents of every dollar generated from stamp sales to the Migratory Bird Conservation Fund. This fund, administered by the USFWS, is utilized for the acquisition or leasing of critical wetland and grassland habitats for inclusion in the National Wildlife Refuge System and other protected areas. These protected lands not only serve as essential breeding, resting, and wintering grounds for migratory waterfowl but also support hundreds of other species of birds, mammals, fish, and plants.

Over the decades, the cost of the Federal Duck Stamp has incrementally increased, reflecting rising land values and conservation needs. The current price stands at $25. Despite its origins, the stamp has evolved to garner broader support beyond the hunting community. Birdwatchers, environmentalists, stamp collectors, and outdoor enthusiasts frequently purchase the stamp as a direct contribution to conservation, recognizing its profound impact on preserving natural landscapes and biodiversity.

A unique aspect of the program is its annual art competition. Each year, a different artist's work is selected to adorn the stamp, transforming it into a collectible item and an annual showcase of wildlife art. This tradition further highlights the cultural and ecological significance of the birds and habitats the stamp helps protect.

Now nearing its ninth decade, the Federal Duck Stamp remains one of the most successful conservation programs in U.S. history. Its continued sales are vital for sustaining millions of acres of habitat, supporting diverse wildlife populations, and providing public access for various outdoor recreational activities. The program exemplifies a model where user fees directly fund the protection of shared natural resources, ensuring the long-term health of critical ecosystems and the species dependent on them.
