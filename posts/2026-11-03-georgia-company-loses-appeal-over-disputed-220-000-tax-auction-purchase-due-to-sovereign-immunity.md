Atlanta, Georgia – A Georgia-based company, F.A.I.T.H. LLC, lost an appeal on October 30, 2023, concerning a $220,000 tax auction purchase for an Atlanta parcel it alleges does not exist. The Georgia Court of Appeals affirmed Fulton County's protection under sovereign immunity, blocking the company's attempt to reclaim its investment.

The dispute stems from F.A.I.T.H. LLC’s acquisition of a tax lien for a specific property during a Fulton County auction. Following the purchase, the company asserted that the parcel described in the auction, located in Atlanta, was an "illusory property" and did not physically exist. Efforts by F.A.I.T.H. LLC to recoup the $220,000 paid were met with the county's defense of sovereign immunity, a legal doctrine that shields government entities from most lawsuits unless they explicitly consent to be sued or waive their immunity.

Sovereign immunity plays a critical role in limiting the financial liability of government bodies. In this instance, the Georgia Court of Appeals upheld the principle that Fulton County is immune from claims for damages sought by F.A.I.T.H. LLC. The court's decision reinforces the established legal framework that governs interactions between private entities and state or local governments in Georgia.

Details surrounding the purchased parcel indicate it was described in county records as "0.00 acres," a detail that F.A.I.T.H. LLC cited in its argument that the property lacked tangible existence. The company sought to void the sale and recover its funds, contending that a transaction involving a non-existent asset should be invalidated. However, the legal precedent upheld by the appeals court emphasizes the responsibilities of bidders in tax lien auctions, often operating under the principle of "caveat emptor," or buyer beware.

Tax lien auctions are designed to recover outstanding property taxes, with buyers typically acquiring the right to collect delinquent taxes plus interest, or eventually gain ownership of the property if taxes remain unpaid. Participants are generally expected to conduct thorough due diligence on properties before bidding. This includes verifying the property's existence, boundaries, and any encumbrances. The current ruling suggests that even in cases where the physical existence of a property is disputed, the shield of sovereign immunity can prevent a county from being held liable for monetary claims by the buyer.

The ruling has implications for future participants in Georgia's tax lien auctions, underscoring the necessity of comprehensive research and risk assessment prior to bidding. While F.A.I.T.H. LLC's specific legal recourse following this appeal decision remains to be fully determined, it highlights the challenges private entities face when seeking redress from governmental bodies protected by sovereign immunity. The case serves as a reminder of the inherent risks in these types of investments and the robust legal protections afforded to governmental jurisdictions.
