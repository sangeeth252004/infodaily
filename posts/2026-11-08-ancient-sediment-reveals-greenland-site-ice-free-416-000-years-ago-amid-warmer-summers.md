A recent scientific study, utilizing a sediment core from a former United States Cold War military base in northwest Greenland, has revealed that the site was ice-free approximately 416,000 years ago. The research, published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* (PNAS), indicates that summers during this interglacial period were significantly warmer, approximately 4.7°C above pre-industrial levels, leading to a substantial reduction or complete disappearance of the Greenland Ice Sheet in the region.

The findings challenge previous assumptions about the long-term stability of the Greenland Ice Sheet, suggesting it is more vulnerable to warming temperatures than previously understood. This period, known as Marine Isotope Stage 11 (MIS 11), occurred between 424,000 and 374,000 years ago and is characterized by a warmer climate. The study’s lead authors, Professor Paul Bierman and Andrew Christ from the University of Vermont, along with an international team of collaborators, analyzed the D-1 core, which was originally retrieved in 1966 from beneath Camp Century.

The sediment core, rediscovered in a freezer at the University of Copenhagen in 2017, contains preserved plant material and other geological markers that provide concrete evidence of an ice-free landscape. Analysis of the core using advanced dating techniques, including luminescence dating and cosmogenic nuclide dating, determined the age of the sediments and indicated exposure to sunlight and liquid water. These conditions are inconsistent with a permanent, overlying ice sheet.

During MIS 11, global sea levels were estimated to be 5-6 meters higher than present-day levels. The new research supports the hypothesis that a significantly diminished or absent Greenland Ice Sheet played a substantial role in this ancient sea level rise. This historical data provides a critical natural analogue for understanding the potential impacts of current and future anthropogenic climate change.

Key details from the study include:

*   **Core Origin:** The D-1 core was extracted in 1966 from beneath Camp Century, a top-secret US Army base constructed within the Greenland Ice Sheet.
*   **Rediscovery:** After decades, the core was rediscovered in a Danish freezer in 2017, prompting new analysis.
*   **Timeframe:** Sediment analysis dated the ice-free period to approximately 416,000 years ago, during Marine Isotope Stage 11.
*   **Temperature Anomaly:** Summers during this period were calculated to be approximately 4.7°C warmer than pre-industrial temperatures.
*   **Sea Level Impact:** Global sea levels during MIS 11 were 5-6 meters higher than today, with a substantially reduced Greenland Ice Sheet contributing to this elevation.
*   **Implication:** The study demonstrates that the Greenland Ice Sheet has been highly susceptible to past warming events, underscoring its potential instability in a warming world.

The insights gained from this ancient geological record are crucial for refining climate models and improving predictions of future sea level rise. As global temperatures continue to rise, understanding the historical behavior of major ice sheets like Greenland's becomes increasingly vital for assessing future climate risks and informing adaptation strategies worldwide. Further research into similar paleoclimate records is expected to continue informing projections for planetary ice melt and sea level changes.
