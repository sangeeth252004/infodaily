In a recent submission to the Supreme Court of India, the Delhi Police stated that it denied permission for a protest at Jantar Mantar, New Delhi, citing the "past conduct" of the Citizens for Justice and Peace (CJP). The police's statement came as part of ongoing legal proceedings, where the denial of permission for public assembly was reportedly being challenged.

Jantar Mantar is a prominent site in the heart of Delhi, officially designated for protests and demonstrations. Organizations and individuals frequently apply for permits to stage gatherings there to voice grievances or advocate for causes, leveraging its symbolic importance and central location. The denial of permission for such an assembly, particularly when the reasons are presented before the nation's highest court, underscores the complexities surrounding the fundamental right to protest and the state's prerogative to maintain public order.

The Delhi Police's submission specifically indicated that its decision was based on CJP's previous actions or involvement, although the precise nature of the "past conduct" was not detailed in initial reports of the court filing. This reason for denial raises questions regarding the criteria used by authorities to assess applications for public gatherings and the extent to which an organization's history can influence future permissions. The Citizens for Justice and Peace is a non-governmental organization known for its work in civil rights and social justice.

The process of obtaining permission for protests in India involves various steps, including applications to local police authorities who evaluate potential impacts on public order, traffic, and security. Denials can often lead to legal challenges, with petitioners asserting their right to peaceful assembly under the Indian Constitution. The Supreme Court often serves as the final arbiter in such disputes, balancing individual liberties against state interests.
